Harmonic phase watermark

A repeater throws away a CTCSS tone at its 300 Hz filter and makes up its own. It can't throw away your voice. So the ID goes into the voice: the sender locks to the talker's pitch and turns the phase of the odd harmonics between 500 and 2600 Hz, four bits per 32 ms of vowel, carrying a repeating frame with a callsign and a one-byte "tone" code.

Everything below runs here, in WebAssembly built from the C port of the prototype: sotto's pitch tracker, the sender, a two-hop FM repeater simulation, and the receiver. Record yourself or open a file, and the receiver shows what it reads as the audio plays. sotto main page.

    The receiver never sees the sender's pitch track, the slot timing or the bits. Every 256 ms it re-reads all the audio heard so far: it tracks the pitch, finds the slot grid and the filters' phase bend blindly, and tries all 124 codeword starts. It counts an ID once the same frame decodes twice running. It works ahead of the playhead, and the display follows what had been heard at each moment. Files you open or record never leave the browser.

    Does the ID get through?

    Seconds of talking until the receiver had the ID, and the share of raw slot bits it read wrong. The Python columns are the prototype on the 8 kHz clips. The browser column is this page's C port on whatever voice is selected above; run the sweep to fill it.

    Unmarked speech gave no ID, right or wrong, in any of the prototype's 14 channel runs. Narrowband PESQ of marked against original is 4.07 (male) and 3.82 (female). PESQ barely hears phase, so trust your ears over it.

    How it works

    1. sotto's pitch tracker follows the fundamental, F0. The sender tracks the 300-3000 Hz version of its own speech, which is what a receiver will hear, and marks only where that agrees with the full-band pitch.
    2. Each harmonic k is mixed down by k·F0 and low-passed at 30 Hz, giving its slowly varying amplitude and phase.
    3. For an odd harmonic and its even neighbours, the second phase difference ignores timing, delay and small pitch errors. The sender rotates the odd harmonic by at most 90° to set it to 0° (bit 0) or 180° (bit 1). The even harmonics stay as the reference.
    4. Unvoiced slots carry nothing and the receiver treats them as erasures, so a voicing disagreement never slips the bit stream. The frame is 56 bits (base-40 callsign, tone byte, CRC-16) under a K=7 rate-1/2 code, whitened, spread over the slots and sent over and over.

    Dk = arg( zk+1 · zk−1 / zk² ) = ψk+1 − 2ψk + ψk−1  →  0° or 180°

    Natural speech leans Dk toward 0°, which looks like a run of zero bits. Without the whitening, unmarked speech decoded into frames near the all-zero codeword and gave false IDs.

    What it can't do yet

    • Pauses and unvoiced sounds carry nothing: about 40% of slots carry data, so the four bits per slot come to roughly 30-50 raw bits per second while someone talks.
    • The rotation has to be the full amount. At 60% strength the speech's own phase pattern leaks through and a repeater pushes bit errors past 15%; at 40% even a clean wire gives 25%.
    • The receiver re-reads everything it has heard each time, with zero-phase filters. A radio needs a causal tracker and filters, with about 50 ms of delay.
    • It has only been through simulated radios. The next step is an over-the-air test on 70 cm through the Pluto SDR.
